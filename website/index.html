Does the gender equity mismatch explain Korea's fertility collapse?

The most cited explanation says that fertility falls furthest where women gain equality at school and work while the home stays traditional. I tested it against Korea's own history and against 30 OECD countries.

Isaac Saxonov

0.72
Births per woman in South Korea in 2023, the lowest rate ever recorded for a country and a third of what it was in 1980.

A country that modernized at work but not at home

Peter McDonald's gender equity mismatch hypothesis is the standard account of very low fertility. Institutions that treat people as individuals, such as schools and employers, opened to women, while the family did not. Women are asked to work like men and keep house like their mothers, so they have fewer children, or none at all.

Korea is the extreme case, and it has the configuration the theory describes. Korean women enroll and work at close to the rate Korean men do, while Korean men do 18.6% of the unpaid work at home. If the theory is right anywhere, it should be right here.

Two units of analysis

Korea year by year, from 1980 to 2024, to see whether the mismatch tracks the decline, and then 30 OECD countries, one row each, to see whether the size of the gap separates countries with low fertility from ordinary ones. Restricting the comparison to OECD members holds income, health systems and access to contraception roughly constant.

Korea's own history cannot answer the question

The problem is that everything trends at once. Fertility falls while every equity measure rises, and any two smooth trends will correlate strongly whether or not they have anything to do with each other.

Over the period the equity data covers, both spheres improved. Women's labor force participation rose, tertiary enrollment passed 100% of the age group, men's share of housework doubled, and fathers went from 3% of parental leave takers to 32%, all while fertility kept falling.

Correlate the raw yearly values and every equity measure lines up with fertility at between −0.7 and −0.99, which taken literally would say that equality causes fertility to fall. The standard fix is to correlate changes from one year to the next instead, since that removes the shared trend, and once it is removed the correlations are close to zero.

Across countries the mismatch points the right way, but weakly

The relationship runs in the direction the theory predicts, but it is small, and about half of it comes from Korea by itself.

Measuring each sphere in absolute terms, as how far it sits from equality rather than how far it has moved from a country's own past, makes countries comparable on the gap the theory is about. The cluster of countries with the lowest fertility does sit on the high mismatch side, but so do Portugal, Ireland and New Zealand, which have ordinary fertility.

A different variable does most of the work

The weak result raised a question about mechanism. In Korea almost every birth happens inside marriage, so an unequal home is a reason not to marry, and not marrying means no children. In France or Sweden a couple can have children without marrying. If that is how the mismatch operates, it should matter most where births require marriage.

So I added the share of births that occur outside marriage, and it turned out to predict fertility far better than the mismatch does.

Putting both in one regression shows which one matters. Adding the marriage variable raises explained variation from 3% to 28%, and the mismatch coefficient drops to almost zero. The interaction the theory predicts, that an unequal home should cost more where children require marriage, is estimated at zero, and not merely too noisy to distinguish from zero.

The most interesting number is the one nothing explains

With both variables in the model, the model predicts 1.11 births per woman for Korea, and the actual figure is 0.75. It is by far the lowest of the countries where birth is tied to marriage, since Japan and Greece are at 1.20 and Türkiye at 1.50.

The shortfall is not only Korea's. Spain misses by 0.32, Estonia by 0.28 and Finland by 0.19, and none of those is a country where birth is tied to marriage. Whatever separates very low fertility from ordinary fertility among rich countries, three quarters of it is not in this model.

The mismatch is real in Korea, but it is not what makes Korea different

  1. Korea's own history cannot test the hypothesis. Every equity measure trends against fertility over the same decades, and once the shared trend is removed there is nothing left.
  2. Across the OECD the mismatch is weak, and half of it is Korea. The direction is right and it holds after controlling for income, but several countries with large mismatches have perfectly ordinary fertility.
  3. Whether birth is tied to marriage predicts fertility much better. Once it is in the model the mismatch adds nothing, and the interaction the theory predicts is estimated at exactly zero.
  4. None of this refutes the hypothesis. Korea does have the configuration the theory describes, and the mismatch could still work through marriage itself, but if it did it should predict fertility among the countries where birth is tied to marriage, and it does not.
Caveats. Thirty countries is a small sample, and three of them (Korea, Türkiye and Mexico) move the fit noticeably. The OECD time use surveys are one per country and the database does not return the survey year, so their values are of unknown and differing age. Births outside marriage is defined slightly differently for Australia, Japan, Korea and New Zealand, and the Korean housework and leave series are short. A high share of births outside marriage shows how closely birth and marriage are linked in a country, and it is safer to read it that way than as a cause.
